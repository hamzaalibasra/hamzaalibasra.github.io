Verify a Story Before You Publish It — Checklist

The verification system behind MythPost — Hamza's viral-stories site, built on the tagline 'The Truth Behind Every Story': the two-signal test, the VERDICT box, and the checks every story passes before it goes live.

MythPost publishes fast — a hot story goes live within an hour or two of the trend breaking — but speed never outruns verification. Every story on the site carries a VERDICT box that says plainly whether the claim going around is true, a myth, half true, or not proven yet, and every story is checked against the same test before it is published: at least two independent signals, a primary source, and a picture that is real. This checklist is that test, in one page.

Before you believe the story

  • Wait for a second signal. One post, one screenshot, or one small site is not a story — Hamza's rule needs two independent signals before a story is even picked up.
  • Check the primary source first: the original video, the court filing, the company's own statement, the data itself. A report about a source is not the source.
  • Read past the headline. If the article's own body does not support its headline, the headline is the story — and it is a false one.
  • Check the date. An old clip, an old quote, or an old photo dressed as today's news is the most common way a false story travels.
  • Ask who benefits if you believe it. A story that flatters an agenda, sells a product, or punishes a rival deserves one extra check, not one fewer.

Check the claim, not the crowd

  • Separate the claim from the reaction. Millions of shares prove a story is popular, not that it is true.
  • Search for the opposite: what do the people denying it say, and what evidence do they have? A claim with no serious challenger is either solid or unexamined.
  • If the numbers are the story, check the numbers: the original table, the filing, the dataset — not a screenshot of someone else's summary.
  • Name the source in the story itself, with a link a reader can open. A source you cannot show is a source you do not have.
  • If the claim cannot be proven either way, say so plainly. 'Not proven yet' is an honest verdict; a guess dressed as fact is not.

Build the story honestly

  • Give every story a verdict: IT'S TRUE, IT'S A MYTH, HALF TRUE, or NOT PROVEN YET — the claim people are repeating against the facts you actually verified.
  • Write the timeline: what happened, in order, with dates. A story without a timeline is a rumour with good formatting.
  • Say what happens next. A verified story tells the reader what to watch for — the hearing, the statement, the data release — instead of ending on a cliffhanger.
  • Use a real photograph, never a generated image. If the first source fails, fall back in order — Wikimedia, Openverse, Pexels or Pixabay, NASA — but never publish a picture that pretends to show something it does not.
  • Play-test every embedded video before publishing, and never remove a video a story already shipped with. A dead embed is a broken promise to the reader.

Before you hit publish

  • Read the headline as a search question: does it ask what a real person would type, and does the story answer it in the first two lines?
  • Check the lead gives the point in two lines. If the reader has to scroll to learn what happened, the lead has failed.
  • Re-check every number in the story against its source, one last time. A single wrong figure undoes a whole verified story.
  • Confirm the verdict still matches the evidence after every edit. A story edited past its verdict is a story that lies politely.
  • If anything in the checklist failed, hold the story. Being second and right beats being first and wrong — every time.
