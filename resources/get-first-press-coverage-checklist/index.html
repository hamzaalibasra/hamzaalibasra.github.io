Get Your First Press Coverage — Checklist

How Hamza earned his first four press features — ValiantCEO, Starter Story, CanvasRebel, and Leaders Perception — without paying for any of them: three routes, one honest pitch, and the discipline to follow up the same day.

Nobody covers a promise. Hamza's first four press features — ValiantCEO in October, Starter Story in mid-November, then CanvasRebel and Leaders Perception in the same week — all came after the work existed: four live websites with real numbers behind them. Interviews alone turned out to be the weak route, so the system below runs on three stronger ones instead: expert quotes inside other people's articles, honest reviews of the sites themselves, and data stories built from numbers only he has. Nothing here is paid for, and nothing here promises an editor's timing — because no honest checklist can.

Before you pitch anyone

  • Have something real to be covered for: a built thing with numbers a stranger can check. Coverage follows work; it does not precede it.
  • Write your one-line identity and use it everywhere, word for word. Hamza's is: Founder of Grab AI Tool, ToolPixi & DailyWallPix.
  • Gather the facts an editor would verify: launch dates, real counts, real revenue if you have it. Every number must survive a check.
  • Keep one press page of your own — bio, portrait, and the coverage you already have — so an editor can verify you in one click.
  • Decide now: you will never pay for coverage. A paid feature is an ad, and it buys you nothing with the readers who matter.

Three routes that actually work

  • Expert quotes: answer real journalist requests on HARO and Source of Sources, in your own words, inside someone else's article. A quote that gets cited beats an interview that gets skimmed.
  • Site reviews: pitch the thing you built for an honest review. Editors cover products their readers would actually use.
  • Data stories: publish a number only you can produce. Hamza's first data asset was Grab's price tracker — 10 of 12 tracked AI tools had raised their prices — a fact no one else had.
  • Run all three routes at once. One route is a hope; three routes are a system.
  • Send in small rounds — about seven pitches at a time — so every reply gets a proper answer.

The pitch itself

  • Lead with the person, not the product: who you are, what you built, and the one number that proves it is real.
  • Keep it short enough to read on a phone. If the editor has to scroll to find the point, the point is lost.
  • Offer one clear angle: the quote, the review, or the data. Never ask an editor to invent your story for you.
  • Never promise a date, a count, or a placement. Editors decide timing; your job is to be worth choosing.
  • Write it yourself, in your own voice. A pitch that sounds like everyone else's reads like everyone else's.

After you send it

  • Follow up the same day you are asked — never staggered, never late. A fast answer is half the impression.
  • Log every pitch, every reply, and every live link in one place, with dates. The log is what stops you pitching the same editor twice.
  • When a feature goes live, link it from your own press page the same day, and thank the editor in one short message.
  • If a route goes quiet twice, change the route — not the volume. More of a dead route is still dead.
  • Remember what interviews taught the hard way: being quoted inside someone else's article carries further than talking about yourself. Build assets worth citing, and keep building them.
