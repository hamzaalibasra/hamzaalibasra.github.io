Your Competition Is Free School

Most founders stare at competitors with anxiety or envy. The smart ones treat them as a free education — and graduate with better ideas.

Share this insight Share on X Facebook WhatsApp LinkedIn

Most founders look at competitors the wrong way. Some look with fear, like the rival is a storm coming to wipe them out. Some look with envy, counting someone else's wins like they are their losses. And some look with a copier, rebuilding feature for feature, convinced that imitation is a strategy. It is not. Copying gets you their yesterday while they build their tomorrow.

I look at competitors like a free school. Every serious rival is running experiments in public — on pricing, on product, on messaging, on what the customer actually cares about — and publishing the results in real time. Their wins are case studies. Their mistakes are lessons you never had to pay for. All it costs you is attention and a notebook.

Here is what I actually study. Their reviews, because customer complaints are free market research — people will tell you, in plain words, exactly what the incumbent fails at, which is your opening. Their pricing moves, because a price change tells you what the market is finally willing to pay for. Their hiring, because job postings are a map of what they are building next. And their messaging shifts, because when a company rewrites its story, the old one stopped working.

But there is a line most founders cross without noticing: the difference between stealing a lesson and stealing a look. The lesson is that customers want faster onboarding, or honest pricing, or human support. The look is their color scheme, their copy, their feature list. Take the lesson, leave the look. Then solve the same problem in your own voice, with your own standard. The market does not need a second version of anyone. It needs the thing nobody built yet.

Keep perspective, too. The rival you are most afraid of is serving slightly different people with slightly different problems, with different resources and different timing. Their moves are data about them, not verdicts about you. Obsess over customers, study competitors — one tells you what to build, the other shows you how not to build it.

So enroll in the school your competitors are running for free. Read their launches like textbooks. Read their failures like warnings. Then close the notebook and build something they cannot copy from you — because by the time they read your playbook, you will already be on the next lesson.
